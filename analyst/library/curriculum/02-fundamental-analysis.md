# 02: Fundamental analysis: what a business is worth and what the market expects

## 1. The one equation
**Price = Earnings × Multiple.** A stock goes up if earnings rise, if the multiple
the market pays for those earnings rises, or both. Every thesis is a claim about one or
both. Write down which.

- **Earnings growth** comes from revenue growth (volume × price), margin expansion
  (operating leverage, cost cuts, mix), and share count reduction (buybacks).
- **Multiple** expands when growth accelerates, quality or visibility improves, rates fall,
  or sentiment turns. It contracts on the reverse. Multiple swings often dominate in the short run.

## 2. Expectations: where the money is
- The market prices **consensus estimates**. A beat that's smaller than the "whisper"
  can sell off. A miss that's better than feared can rally.
- **Estimate revisions are among the most robust signals in equities.** Stocks whose
  forward estimates are being raised tend to keep outperforming. Downward revisions
  tend to persist (analysts revise gradually). When you research a name, find out whether
  estimates are going up or down, via news or a web search ("[ticker] estimate revisions",
  "[ticker] raised guidance").
- **Guidance > the quarter.** The stock reacts to next quarter and full-year guidance far
  more than to the just-reported numbers. Guidance raised while the street is too low is
  the strongest setup in fundamental trading.
- **Post-earnings announcement drift (PEAD):** after a big positive (negative) surprise, the
  stock tends to keep drifting in that direction for weeks. It is one of the most
  documented anomalies. See playbook 05 (A).

## 3. Valuation toolkit (relative, never absolute)
| Metric | Use for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Forward P/E | Profitable companies | Compare to its own history, peers, and growth. |
| PEG (P/E ÷ growth) | Growth names | Around 1 or below is often reasonable, but only if the growth is durable. |
| EV/EBITDA | Capital-intensive, leveraged companies | Accounts for debt. Good for industrials, energy, telecom. |
| EV/Sales | Unprofitable / early growth | Weakest anchor. Pair it with gross margin and growth rate ("Rule of 40" for software: growth % + FCF margin % ≥ 40 is healthy). |
| P/B, P/TBV | Banks, insurers | Pair with ROE. A high ROE justifies a higher P/B. |
| FCF yield | Everyone, especially mature | FCF / market cap. Real cash, harder to fake than earnings. |
| Dividend yield | Staples, utilities, telecom, REITs (use FFO/AFFO for REITs) | Compare to the 10-year yield. They compete. |

Valuation alone is a terrible timing tool. Cheap stocks get cheaper ("value trap") and
expensive stocks get more expensive. Use valuation to judge **asymmetry** (how far it
can fall if wrong) and **catalysts** to judge timing.

## 4. Quality: what separates compounders from traps
- **Returns on capital** (ROIC/ROE) consistently above cost of capital indicate a moat.
- **Gross margin** level and trend show pricing power. Falling gross margins are an early warning.
- **Free cash flow conversion** (FCF ÷ net income) near or above 1 is healthy. Persistently low
  conversion means earnings may not be real cash (watch receivables and inventories growing
  faster than sales).
- **Balance sheet:** net debt/EBITDA (over ~3–4× is risky outside utilities/REITs), interest
  coverage, debt maturities. Leveraged companies are fragile when rates rise or growth slows.
- **Share count:** steady decline means buybacks. Rising share count means dilution (common in
  unprofitable tech; heavy stock-based comp is a real cost).
- **Management & capital allocation:** buybacks at sensible prices, disciplined M&A, honest guidance.

## 5. Reading filings fast (SEC EDGAR via WebSearch/WebFetch)
- **10-K:** Business section (how they make money), Risk Factors (skim for what's new
  vs last year), MD&A (management's explanation of trends), segment data.
- **10-Q:** quarterly update. Check segment trends, guidance changes, liquidity.
- **8-K:** material events: earnings releases (Item 2.02), executive departures (5.02),
  acquisitions, material agreements. A surprise CFO departure is a classic red flag.
- **Earnings call transcripts / press releases:** tone on demand, pricing, margins, and
  what analysts keep asking about (that's what the market is worried about).
- **Form 4:** insider transactions. Cluster *buying* by multiple insiders is meaningful.
  Routine selling is usually noise.
- **13F:** large holders' quarterly positions. Lagging and low signal. Skip unless relevant.

## 6. Earnings season: how to play it
- Know the earnings date of every holding (web search "[ticker] earnings date"). Decide
  deliberately before the print. Holding through earnings is a binary bet: size it as one.
- Typical earnings-day moves for large caps run several percent, and much more for
  high-growth or controversial names. If you hold a 10% position, a 15% gap is a 1.5% hit
  to the account.
- Early reporters (big banks, then mega-cap tech) set the tone for their sectors.
  Read-throughs: a supplier's guide tells you about customers, and vice versa (TSMC/ASML
  results read into NVDA/AMD/AMAT/LRCX; railroads and FDX/UPS read into the industrial economy).
- After the print, judge the **reaction**, not the report. A stock that rallies on bad news
  has sellers exhausted. A stock that falls on great news is crowded.

## 7. Sector-specific lenses (details in the dossiers)
- **Tech/semis:** revenue growth, gross margin, data-center/AI demand, inventory cycles,
  capex guides of the hyperscalers (their capex is the semis' revenue).
- **Software:** net revenue retention, remaining performance obligations, billings, FCF margin.
- **Banks:** net interest income/margin, loan growth, deposit costs, credit provisions,
  CET1 capital, investment-banking/trading fees.
- **Retail/consumer:** same-store sales (traffic vs ticket), gross margin, inventory health.
- **Industrials:** orders and backlog, book-to-bill, pricing vs cost.
- **Energy:** realized commodity prices, production, capex discipline, buybacks and dividends.
- **Pharma/biotech:** pipeline readouts, patent cliffs, launches (script trends), pricing policy.
- **Staples:** organic growth split into price vs volume. Volume declines while price
  still rises mean the pricing power is running out.
